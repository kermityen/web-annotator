Baking basics

Why your sourdough starter needs a schedule

6 min read

A sourdough starter is a small ecosystem of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria. Feed it flour and water, and those microbes eat, multiply, and produce the gas that makes bread rise.

Most problems with a starter come from inconsistency rather than from the recipe. When feedings happen at random times, the yeast never settles into a predictable rhythm, and the bread inherits that unpredictability.

The simplest fix is to feed at the same time every day and keep the jar somewhere with a steady temperature. Around 24°C, a healthy starter should roughly double within four to six hours.

Reading the signs

A starter at its peak has a domed top, a web of bubbles along the glass, and a mild, yogurt-like smell. If it collapses before you use it, it has already run out of food.

A layer of grey liquid on top is a sign of hunger, not spoilage. Pour it off, feed the starter, and give it a day or two of regular feedings before baking with it again.

Once the rhythm is steady, you can slow everything down by moving the jar to the fridge between bakes and feeding it once a week.
